Optical NDVI and Sentinel-1 RVI were extracted over the same area for 2019 to 2020 and both show two growing seasons per year. NDVI is lowest in the dry season (0.31 in March 2020), rises as crops are planted and grow to a first peak in June to July (about 0.65), falls to a dip in October (0.53 to 0.55) as the first crop is harvested, then rises to a second peak in mid-November (0.59 to 0.67). The radar curve follows the same cycle: RVI is lowest in late April (0.63), when fields are bare or newly planted, then rises as the canopy develops, peaking in July to August (about 0.74) and again from December to January (0.77), with a dip in October (0.69 to 0.70) after harvest.

The radar signal reflects structural change in the canopy. Bare soil scatters the radar signal weakly and RVI is low. As leaves, stems and biomass build up, volume scattering increases and RVI rises, and after harvest the scattering falls again. RVI lags NDVI by roughly one to eight weeks, because NDVI responds to leaf greenness, which peaks first, while RVI responds to biomass and structure, which keep building. NDVI has the larger seasonal range (0.31 to 0.67 against 0.63 to 0.77 for RVI), so it separates growth stages more sharply. Radar complements optical because it is unaffected by cloud: in the rainy season, when optical images are scarce, the RVI curve stays continuous and confirms that the same planting, growth and harvest stages occurred. The standard phenology table was unreliable for radar because its main peak falls on the year boundary, so peaks and troughs were read directly from the smoothed curves.
